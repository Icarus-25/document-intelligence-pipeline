## Informational Interview

### Interview Subject Profile

For this informational interview, I spoke with Alex Martinez, a Senior Software Engineer at AeroTech Solutions, a mid-sized aviation technology company specializing in plane diagnostics and predictive maintenance tools. Alex has over seven years of professional experience in software engineering, beginning as a junior backend developer before moving into cloud systems and machines learning integration for aviation data pipelines. He earned his bachelor’s degree in Computer Science from the University of Washington and later completed a professional certificate in cloud architecture.

I connected with Alex through a mutual contact in my work network at SpaceX. I chose to interview him because his work directly intersects two areas I am interested in: aviation systems and software engineering. Additionally, Alex transitioned into aviation tech from a more general software background, and I wanted to learn how he navigated that shift. His path and experience offered valuable insight into the type of work I hope to pursue as I develop my own technical skills.

### Interview Questions &amp; Summary Responses

1. How did you get started in your current role?
Alex explained that he began his career as a backend engineer in the e-commerce sector, where he worked primarily on user authentication systems and transaction processing. Over time, he realized he wanted to move toward more meaningful, data-driven work that involved solving complex real-world problems. He spent a year building his skill set by learning about distributed systems, containerization, and cloud platforms, especially AWS and Kubernetes. With these skills, he applied to AeroTech Solutions even though he had little aviation background. The company was more interested in his strong fundamentals, curiosity, and ability to learn quickly. Alex shared that the transition was challenging but rewarding, and that the company provided plenty of onboarding support to help him understand aviation-specific concepts.

2. What does a typical day look like?
According to Alex, his day begins with reviewing system logs and monitoring dashboards to ensure their data ingestion pipelines run smoothly overnight. Any anomalies—like sudden spikes in sensor data or failures in batch jobs—are addressed at once. Late mornings are spent in stand-up meetings where the team reviews ongoing projects, roadblocks, and upcoming deployments. Afternoons usually revolve around writing and testing code, performing peer code reviews, and taking part in architecture or design discussions. He emphasized that collaboration is constant, especially because his team works with data scientists, product managers, and aviation safety specialists. He noted that context-switching is common, but it keeps the work interesting and dynamic.

3. What skills are most important in your role?
Alex highlighted several technical and soft skills. On the technical side, ability in Python, distributed systems, data streaming tools, and cloud infrastructure—particularly AWS Lambda, EC2, S3, and Step Functions—are essential. He also emphasized debugging skills, explaining that aviation data is messy, and pinpointing subtle issues requires patience and deep system understanding. In terms of soft skills, he stressed the importance of communication because his role often requires translating engineering problems into language that non-technical stakeholders can understand. The ability to collaborate, give constructive feedback, and think critically are also crucial components of success.

4. What are the biggest challenges you face?
One of the primary challenges he meets is handling massive and inconsistent aviation datasets generated by sensors on planes. These datasets often hold noise, missing values, or unusual spikes that must be cleaned before analysis. Another challenge is supporting system reliability—since the telemetry pipeline is used by airline operators, even minor disruptions could cause delays or misinformation. Alex also pointed out the complexity of integrating legacy aviation systems with newer cloud-based architectures, which requires careful planning and incremental implementation to avoid breaking existing workflows.

5. How do you stay updated with technology trends?
Alex said he treats learning as part of his daily routine. He follows engineering blogs such as AWS Architecture Blog and Medium’s tech publications, takes specialized online courses to keep up with cloud innovations, and attends local meetups focused on backend engineering and aviation technology. He also contributes to open-source tools that his team uses, which he said helps reinforce good engineering habits while staying sharp. His company regularly hosts internal workshops, so he makes a point to attend those as well.

6. What advice would you give someone entering this field?
His main advice is to build strong computer science fundamental data structures, algorithms, networking, and operating systems—because they form the backbone of every engineering decision. He emphasized that early-career engineers should remain curious and experiment with different domains before committing to a specialization. He also recommended working on personal projects that challenge problem-solving abilities and developing a habit of reading others’ code to learn better patterns.

7. What do you wish you had known earlier?
Alex shared that early in his career, he believed writing clever or highly optimized code was the mark of a strong engineer. Over time, he realized that readability and maintainability matter far more. He learned to write code as if the next person keeping it is exhausted and stressed—because sometimes that person is himself after a long week. He emphasized documentation, clear naming, and modular design as skills he wished he had prioritized sooner.

8. What are you currently working on?
Alex is currently leading a long-term initiative to redesign AeroTech’s real-time telemetry pipeline. The project involves moving from a traditional server-based architecture to a serverless, event-driven model using AWS Lambda, Kinesis, and SQS. The goal is to reduce latency, cut bottlenecks during peak transmission hours, and cut operational costs by scaling computer resources automatically. He explained that this project will influence how airlines access diagnostics and maintenance recommendations in real time.

9. How does your team approach problem-solving when dealing with system failures or unexpected issues?
Alex described their approach as structured and calm. When a system failure occurs, the team’s top priority is restoring functionality quickly, even if the root cause is not yet fully understood. After stabilization, they conduct a “blameless post-mortem,” a practice that encourages psychological safety and focuses on systems, not people. In this meeting, they analyze logs, discuss patterns, and outline potential improvements. Each action item is documented and assigned to ensure follow-through. Alex stressed that aviation systems require discipline, collaboration, and levelheadedness, as impulsive changes can worsen the situation.

10. What qualities differentiate a good software engineer from a great one?
Alex believes the difference lies largely in mindset. Good engineers complete their tasks efficiently, but great engineers understand how their work fits into the broader system. They think ahead, predict edge cases, and communicate clearly with peers. Great engineers also ask thoughtful questions and are willing to challenge assumptions respectfully. Alex added that humility and lifelong learning are indispensable qualities—technology changes quickly, and those who adapt continue to grow.

**Notable Quote:** *“Aviation is unforgiving — your software has to be reliable even on its worst day.”*
